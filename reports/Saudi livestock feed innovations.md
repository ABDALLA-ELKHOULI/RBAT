# Waste streams and desert grasses beat moonshot feeds

Saudi Arabia's most realistic new feed opportunities are not the most futuristic ones. The best near-term options are **date by-product feeds** (energy blocks and pellets for camels, sheep and goats), **perennial C4 grasses and spineless cactus on deficit or subsurface drip** for licensed seasonal-fodder farms, and **black soldier fly (BSF) protein built in partnership with poultry integrators**. Each fits the post-2018 green-fodder ban, uses waste or far less water than alfalfa, and has a buyer. Treated-wastewater fodder is technically proven but limited by pathogen and heavy-metal risk. Hydroponic sprouted barley saves water but loses money on a dry-matter basis. Microalgae remains a state-led KAUST pilot. The stakes are large. The Kingdom imports roughly **4.5 Mt of barley, 4.7 Mt of corn and about 0.95 Mt of alfalfa hay a year**, so any local feed competes against more than 9 Mt of imported, dollar-priced grain. Policy explicitly favours low-water, seasonal, modern-irrigated forage, along with waste diversion and 90% poultry self-sufficiency. The main caveat is that almost no audited Saudi unit-cost data exist for any novel feed, so the rankings below rest on water, policy fit and market access more than on proven margins.

## Nine million tonnes of imported grain set the bar

Saudi feed security now depends on imports. USDA forecasts MY2026/27 barley imports at **4.5 MMT**, up from 1.9 MMT in 2024/25, with corn at **4.66 MMT**. The drivers are strategic reserve rebuilding and new feed-mill capacity ([USDA FAS](https://www.fas.usda.gov/data/gain/2026/04/saudi-arabia-grain-and-feed-annual); [World-Grain](https://www.world-grain.com/articles/22195-saudi-arabia-to-import-more-barley-corn)). Alfalfa imports were about **950,000 t worth USD 460M in 2023, 59% of it from the US** ([Tridge](https://www.tridge.com/intelligences/alfalfa/SA/import)). Demand is large: more than **22 million sheep, 7.3 million goats and 2.24 million camels** in 2024, plus a broiler sector that grew 12.9% to 1.3 Mt ([GASTAT](https://stats.gov.sa/en/node/171118); [GASTAT](https://stats.gov.sa/en/w/news/119)).

Policy created this dependence on purpose. The green-fodder ban took effect in November 2018. Green fodder had used about **17 billion m³ of water a year**, and the ban was expected to save about 9 billion m³ ([Argaam](https://www.argaam.com/en/article/articledetail/id/568056)). The implementing mechanism bans alfalfa and Rhodes grass. Farms over 100 ha may grow forage only with modern irrigation, on crops and areas MEWA specifies, and fodder grown abroad for the Kingdom is encouraged ([Qanoniah](https://qanoniah.com/en/File/Jy4DGw9da7vj1vg78Qv65RrKX-Mechanism-for-Implementing-Regulations-on-Halting-the-Cultivation-of-Green-Fodder)). Violators face fines of **SAR 4,000/ha enforced by aerial surveillance** ([Argaam](https://www.argaam.com/en/article/articledetail/id/612910)). Almarai has sourced 100% of its alfalfa abroad since 2019 ([EFG Hermes](https://www.efghermesone.com/News/Index/almarai-secures-100-of-alfalfa-needs-from-imports)), and FAO reports plans to phase out domestic alfalfa entirely by 2027/28 ([FAO GIEWS](https://www.fao.org/giews/countrybrief/country.jsp?code=SA)).

The policy has since loosened in one important way. A **December 2023 cabinet decision lets companies and large farmers grow seasonal fodder under MEWA controls** ([Argaam](https://www.argaam.com/en/article/articledetail/id/1692596)). In 2025, MEWA issued two-year winter fodder seed licences to Al-Jouf Agricultural and TADCO ([Argaam](https://www.argaam.com/en/article/articledetail/id/1818699)), and perennial fodder is being phased toward seasonal crops capped at 50 ha ([Okaz](https://www.okaz.com.sa/local/na/2232883)). The direction is clear: domestic forage is allowed only if it is licensed, seasonal and water-efficient. The detailed post-2023 rules on regional quotas and metering were not found. Small herders now get **per-head payments rather than import subsidies**, capped at 300 head per category ([millingmea](https://millingmea.com/?p=2027472)), which leaves mid-size operators fully exposed to world prices.

## Buffel grass and cactus grow several times more feed per cubic metre than alfalfa

The strongest Saudi-specific crop evidence favours perennial C4 grasses. The first systematic Saudi evaluation of buffel grass, using northern accessions, found yields of **9.3–18.4 t/ha (mean about 14) on an estimated 400–500 mm of water a year**. The five-year alfalfa average was about 17 t/ha on 2,500–3,000 mm. The Jameen accession yielded the most and resisted overgrazing best, and the Aja accession was the most salt-tolerant ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4395/15/2/371)). Taken at face value, that is roughly 3 kg DM/m³ against alfalfa's 0.6–0.7. A UAE trial measured more conservatively, at **0.7–0.8 kg DM/m³**, still the best of the grasses tested ([IGC 2023](https://uknowledge.uky.edu/igc/XXV_IGC_2023/Ecology/60)). Planners should use the lower figure until Saudi water-use measurements are confirmed. Blue panic, tested in Saudi Arabia, tolerated **15,000 ppm salinity, used almost 50% less water than alfalfa and carried 15–18% crude protein** ([AGJSR](https://agjsr.agu.edu.bh/publications/paper/1097)).

| Option | Saudi/Gulf evidence | Water | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buffel grass | 14 t/ha mean, northern KSA accessions | ~400–500 mm/yr; 0.7–0.8 kg DM/m³ (UAE) | Feed value data for KSA missing |
| Blue panic | 15–18% CP, 15,000 ppm salt tolerance | ~50% less than alfalfa | Old (1988) study |
| Spineless cactus | ICARDA nursery serving KSA; frost tests in north | 2,500 vs 5,000 m³/ha showed no yield difference | ~10% DM, low protein, frost |
| Atriplex/halophytes | 16.7–25.2% CP in KSA-grown species | Best at 2,000 m³/ha drip; brackish OK | High ash, low intake |
| Alfalfa (benchmark) | ~17 t/ha | 2,500–3,000 mm/yr | Banned/phasing out |

Spineless cactus is probably the most water-thrifty option of all. ICARDA's Arabian Peninsula trials found **no yield difference between 2,500 and 5,000 m³/ha/yr**, and they tested frost tolerance in northern Saudi Arabia ([CGIAR MEL](https://repo.mel.cgiar.org/items/35a07403-d2fe-4753-8028-47dbbb14900f)). Rainfed cactus elsewhere reaches **3.3–6.2 kg DM/m³** ([CGIAR](https://mel.cgiar.org/reporting/downloadmelspace/hash/e93bd596c556981ce90316a0662ccb76/v/2f75c6cf9793152c6b6ee91b8595b5b5)). It also cuts sheep drinking water from **2.4 L to 0.1 L a day** ([FAO](https://www.fao.org/4/Y2808E/y2808e0b.htm)), which is valuable in the desert. Its limits are low dry matter and low protein, so it must be fed with fibre and a protein source.

Halophytes belong on brackish blocks, not at the centre of the ration. At King Faisal University, Atriplex halimus and Suaeda gave the best fodder yields, and **drip at 2,000 m³/ha/yr** gave the best yield and quality ([KFU](https://services.kfu.edu.sa/ScientificJournal/en/Home/ContentsDetails/128)). Saudi-grown Atriplex species contain 16.7–25.2% CP ([JRM](https://journals.uair.arizona.edu/index.php/jrm/article/download/7953/7565)). Seawater-irrigated saltbush, however, runs **25–40% ash, and sheep ate only about 400 g DM/day** ([BGU](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/developing-the-seawater-agriculture-concept-2/)). ICBA's Salicornia work stays in the UAE ([ICBA](https://www.biosaline.org/news/2018-05-10-6466)), and no Saudi Salicornia forage project with data was found.

Irrigation method matters as much as crop choice. KSU found that Rhodes grass hay actually **peaked at 80% of crop water demand**, giving 24.7–27.8 t/ha ([KSU PARC](https://parc.ksu.edu.sa/sites/parc.ksu.edu.sa/files/imce_images/response_of_rhodes_grass_to_variable_rate_application_of_irrigation_water_and_fertilizer_nitrogen_1.pdf)). At KAU, subsurface drip gave the best water-use efficiency for alfalfa and blue panic ([AUN](https://b.aun.edu.eg/agriculture/maximizing-productivity-and-water-use-efficiency-alfalfa-under-precise-sub-surface-drip-irrigation)), and deficit irrigation at 85% of field capacity **saved 13% of water for a 12% yield loss** ([AUN](https://b.aun.edu.eg/agriculture/effect-fully-and-limited-irrigation-forage-production-and-irrigation-water-use-alfalfa-medicago)). Solar pumping costs about **half as much as diesel over 30 years** ([KFUPM](https://pure.kfupm.edu.sa/en/publications/techno-economic-study-of-a-solar-photovoltaic-and-diesel-powered-/)). Together these practices make a licensed seasonal-fodder farm both defensible to MEWA and cheaper to run. The weak points are the lack of Saudi feeding-trial data for buffel grass and cactus diets, and the lack of per-hectare establishment costs.

## Date waste is the cheapest feed nobody fully uses

Saudi Arabia produced **more than 1.9 Mt of dates in 2025**. MEWA, the National Center for Palms and Dates and TAQNIA have studied a Qassim plant to turn about **500,000 t of palm waste into roughly 300,000 t/yr of products** ([Arab News PK](https://www.arabnews.pk/saudi-arabia/qassim-reuses-date-palm-by-products-3002496)), though the plant's status is unknown. Cull dates, fronds, date-press residue and seed meal are already traditional feeds ([Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/uses-of-palm-in-saudi-arabia)). In a Saudi trial, date blocks made of **50% dates, 40% wheat bran, 5% urea and 5% cement** were judged safe as a partial feed for young camels ([CIRAD](https://agritrop.cirad.fr/577435)). Camel fattening on molassed-date diets with urea has also been studied ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-british-society-of-animal-science/article/abs/fattening-performance-of-dromedary-camels-fed-a-finishing-diet-containing-molassed-dates-supplemented-with-0-05-or-10-urea/8FA58D7B681F0352B1D2143CC4E3631D)). Elsewhere in the region, palm leaves treated with **4–6% urea replaced Rhodes grass for lambs** ([AAAID](https://www.aaaid.org/?p=3654)), and date-waste blocks keep for 2–3 years ([ICARDA](https://mel.cgiar.org/reporting/download/hash/jxyAthJ9)).

This is the clearest opportunity for a new entrant. It needs no new water, uses a low-cost feedstock concentrated in Qassim and Al-Ahsa, and the technology is simple. It also has a ready market in the camel, sheep and goat herds that per-head subsidies leave exposed to barley prices. A precedent exists: ARASCO priced its "Wafi" compound feed **at least SAR 8 per 50 kg bag below barley** ([millingmea](https://millingmea.com/?p=2027472)), which shows that herders switch for a price gap. The constraint is nutritional. Date products are energy-rich and protein-poor, so products need urea or a protein partner. Seasonal and logistics costs for collecting fronds and seeds have not been published.

## Insects and algae are protein plays still waiting for scale

Novel protein competes with imported soymeal and fishmeal, not with hay. BSF has the clearest path to market because integrators are seeking it. In February 2026, **Tanmiah signed MoUs with PHYLA (BSF larvae from organic waste) and RECYCLEE**, explicitly tied to the 90% poultry self-sufficiency target ([Zawya](https://www.zawya.com/en/press-release/companies-news/tanmiah-partners-with-phyla-and-recyclee-to-bolster-circular-economy-and-food-security-dduxf5pe)). PHYLA's poultry-feed study runs in King Saud University labs with ARASCO and Tanmiah, but it has published no results ([MENA Startup Digest](https://menastartupdigest.com/?p=48315)). History is a warning: AgriProtein's 2017 plan for three Saudi fly farms processing 250 t/day of waste ([Engineering News](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/saudi-arabia-to-build-three-fly-farms-says-agriprotein-2017-03-28)) left no evidence of plants being built. The economics improve if a plant earns waste gate fees as well as feed revenue. More than 90% of Saudi food waste goes to landfill, against a target of **90% landfill diversion by 2040** ([Arab News](https://arabnews.pk/node/1615941)). SFDA's pathway for insect feed was not found, and that is a real regulatory unknown.

Microalgae is further away. KAUST and MEWA's algal program grew more than 20 strains with **over 40% protein in dry biomass** in 3,000 L pilots. A seawater phase of more than 100,000 L using flue-gas CO₂ was expected in 2023, but no later confirmation or economics study was found ([KAUST](https://kaust.edu.sa/news/algal-biotechnologists-at-kaust-are-generating-sustainable-animal-feeds-in-saudi-arabia)). Algae is likely to enter first as aquafeed or a premium additive, not bulk livestock feed. Food-waste-to-feed is at a similar stage. Carbon CPU converts food waste into feed oils ([Arab News](https://arabnews.pk/node/1615941)), and KAUST is piloting conversion of expired dairy and juice ([KAUST](https://www.kaust.edu.sa/news/turning-spoiled-food-waste-into-commercial-products)).

## Hydroponics and wastewater fodder carry hidden costs

Hydroponic barley is the most-marketed "water-free" fodder and the weakest economically. A Saudi study found it **out-yielded field barley and saved water, yet conventional cultivation was more profitable** because of higher fixed and variable costs, and the authors judged it unsuitable for small farms ([Sustainability](https://dx.doi.org/10.3390/su15010822)). Sprouting **burns 7–47% of the seed's dry matter** ([NJAP](https://njap.org.ng/index.php/njap/article/view/6075)), and US benchmarks put sprouts at about **$0.40/lb DM against $0.11 for good hay** ([Iowa State](https://www.iastatedigitalpress.com/air/article/id/6470/)). Because the seed is usually imported barley, hydroponics turns 1 kg of imported grain into less than 1 kg of feed dry matter. It adds no new feed and does nothing for import dependence. It makes sense only as a freshness supplement or in a service model where someone else carries the capital cost.

Treated wastewater (TWW) is more serious. KACST found that TWW supplied enough nutrients to **cut alfalfa fertiliser costs by about 94%** ([KACST/WCSS](https://old.iuss.org/19th%20WCSS/Symposium/pdf/2053.pdf)), and a MEWA-hosted KSU paper analysed the economics of directing TWW to green fodder by region ([MEWA](https://www.mewa.gov.sa/ar/InformationCenter/OpenData/SiteAssets/Pages/OpenDataExamples/The%20economic%20dimension%20of%20directing%20treated%20wastewater%20to%20theproduction%20of%20green%20fodder%20in%20Saudi%20Arabia.pdf)). But a Makkah study found that higher TWW concentrations **raised toxic elements and faecal bacteria** along with yields ([KAU](https://citis.kau.edu.sa/Files/305/Researches/19093_30302.docx)). TWW fodder is therefore a utility-scale, regulator-led opportunity near cities, not a startup play. It works best with crops that are wilted or ensiled before feeding, and it needs monitoring.

## Conclusion

Saudi feed innovation is not mainly a technology race. Policy has already decided that water is the binding constraint, so winning options turn existing assets (date waste, brackish blocks, landfill-bound organics, licensed seasonal-fodder land) into feed without drawing on fossil groundwater. That makes the opportunity mostly about logistics and offtake: collecting palm waste, signing integrator contracts for BSF, and fitting C4 grasses and cactus into MEWA's licensing system. Glamorous options such as algae and hydroponics either lack scale or, in hydroponics' case, recycle imported grain rather than replacing it.

Financing is the gap. The Agricultural Development Fund approved a record **SAR 7.17B in 2024** ([Okaz](https://www.okaz.com.sa/economy/na/2201017)), but its money flows mainly to poultry and feed-import finance ([Arab News](https://arabnews.com/node/2334716/)), and no verified loan line for low-water fodder was found. Before anyone commits capital, the most valuable next step is a same-site Saudi comparison of SAR per kg of digestible DM and protein for date blocks, buffel and cactus rations, BSF meal and imported barley and alfalfa. That number does not yet exist, and it would show whether these policy-favoured options also pay.
